Title: Seeing the High Energy Universe
EventType: Talk

Status: published

EventID: Sakar-2026-MT

The 2026 Nobel prize in physics has just been awarded to Francis Halzen “for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin”. This novel experiment at the South Pole was the first to detect cosmic neutrinos at TeV-PeV energies, and also identify the first candidate source: a flaring blazar 5.7 billion light-years away. This was a breakthrough in real-time multi-messenger astronomy and poses challenges to astrophysical models of such active galactic nuclei. Intriguingly no neutrinos have been detected from gamma-ray bursts, nor were any seen from the binary neutron star merger detected in gravitational waves. Resolving the high energy neutrino sky and furthering our understanding of such extreme environments will require IceCube-Gen2 as well as other experiments around the world to exploit this new window on the Universe. 

**About the speaker:** <br>
Subir Sarkar is Professor Emeritus of Physics at Oxford University. He studied at the Indian Institute of Technology, Kharagpur and obtained his PhD at the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research, Bombay. Since 1990 he has been at the Rudolf Peierls Centre for Theoretical Physics in Oxford, where he headed the Particle Theory Group. He was also visiting Niels Bohr Professor at Copenhagen University. His research interests are at the interface between astrophysics/cosmology and fundamental physics — including cosmic rays, dark matter, dark energy, inflation & neutrinos. He belongs to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and Cherenkov Telescope Array collaborations and also participates in laser plasma experiments and the Rubin/LSST Dark Energy Science Collaboration. He was awarded the 2017 IUPAP-TIFR Homi Bhabha Medal & Prize.
